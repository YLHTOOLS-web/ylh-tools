METHODOLOGICAL NOTE

Why There's No Single Icon Here

The companion Hall of Faces could point to real carved stone — not of YHWH, but of the storm-god iconography around him. This hall can't quite do that: no single, visually standardized Devil had yet emerged during the biblical period, so there's no settled image to show.

What the sources actually give us

Second Temple literature gives us Watchers, Belial, Mastema, Azazel, satans, and other hostile spirits — but as distinct figures belonging to different textual worlds. Surviving Jewish art does not give us securely identified portraits of most of them, much less a standardized image labeled "Satan." The familiar Devil had not yet acquired one universally recognizable face.

Where the horns and hooves actually come from

The familiar horned, cloven-hooved Devil is a medieval European assembly, not an ancient Near Eastern one — and not a clean single borrowing either. It draws on several visual traditions at once: classical satyrs and Pan-like beings, medieval monster iconography, animal symbolism, and biblical or apocalyptic imagery, stitched together well after Revelation made the decisive textual fusion and patristic writers continued assembling the biography in prose. The look came last, and from more than one mythological family.

On the medieval visual invention specifically: Jeffrey Burton Russell, The Devil: Perceptions of Evil from Antiquity to Primitive Christianity (1977) and Richard Raiswell & David R. Winter, eds., The Medieval Devil: A Reader (University of Toronto Press, 2022), both trace this iconographic history directly.
