ICONOGRAPHIC BACKGROUND

What the Stone Record Actually Shows

No surviving anthropomorphic image can presently be identified as YHWH with scholarly confidence. Israelite and Judahite religion was not uniformly imageless — figurines, sacred animals, trees, cherubim, seals, cult stands, and solar imagery are archaeologically abundant — but the existence and form of any YHWH cult statue remain disputed. The figures in the Hall above are original symbolic illustrations, not reconstructions of a lost artifact. What follows are real, physical objects that illuminate the larger West Semitic and imperial visual worlds YHWH's portrayal developed within.

VIEW PHOTOGRAPH ↗
Baal with Thunderbolt (Ugarit stele)

Limestone bas-relief, Temple of Baal, Ras Shamra/Ugarit, Syria, c. 1300–1150 BCE. Louvre, AO 15775. Depicts Baal, not YHWH. Biblical texts assign YHWH several functions and epithets from the West Semitic storm-god complex — cloud-riding, storm manifestation, combat against Sea, divine-warrior kingship — so this stele illustrates the regional iconographic world behind those motifs. It is not evidence that YHWH was pictured in this exact visual form; we have no surviving image of YHWH to compare it to.

Kuntillet ʿAjrud Pithos A (reconstruction)

Painted storage jar, Sinai, c. 8th century BCE — not carved stone, included as one of the most important surviving artifacts combining an inscription naming YHWH with a larger program of drawings, though the inscription and the adjacent figures may have been added at different times and need not interpret one another. Inscription reads "...by YHWH of Samaria and his asherah" — whether "his asherah" names the goddess Asherah, her cultic object, or a conceptually entangled form of both remains debated. The standing figures are separately contested: once read as Yahweh and Asherah, most current analyses identify them as the Egyptian god Bes. This image is a modern digital redrawing of the faded original, not a period photograph or excavation photo — treat it as illustrative only.

Digital reconstruction: Wikimedia contributor "Amplifysound," public domain

Also relevant for regional context: the Metropolitan Museum's Winged Genius relief panel from Ashurnasirpal II's Northwest Palace at Nimrud (Neo-Assyrian, c. 883–859 BCE). Neo-Assyrian palace reliefs like this show how supernatural beings, kingship, and protection were given monumental visual form under empire — useful background, but not portraits of YHWH and not a model for the Hall's featureless figures above.
